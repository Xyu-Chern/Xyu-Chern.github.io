Robot manipulation / execution-aware control

The Space to React

Before a robot moves, account for the motion it has already promised to execute.

Xu Chen · September–November 2026 · PyBullet research notes

A robot sees a moving barrier and decides to wait. But the commands already sent to its motors keep running. In assembly, the same problem appears in a different form: a correction based on the current pose may be out of date by the time it reaches the gripper.

We study how to make decisions with those pending commands in view. The practical lesson is to separate late intervention, crossing-time prediction, and command handoff. These require different fixes; a single success-rate number hides the distinction.

Three failures that look like one

A failed delivery is not a diagnosis. We first separated three mechanisms using 6,240 fixed-IK simulations: a sweep of waiting actions and a separate intervention on the transition from crossing to placement. The counts below belong to that diagnostic protocol, not the later 48-scene online test.

The first two rows partition the 53 direct-crossing failures. The third uses a different denominator—candidate trajectories—and must not be added to those scene counts. New commands cannot undo a contact that occurs in an immutable prefix. For recoverable scenes, the question becomes whether the controller predicts the crossing window accurately and finishes the handoff before lowering and releasing the object.

Why did better obstacle prediction alone not solve it?

On 48 HOLD scenes, queue-aware motion prediction selected successful waits in 33 cases. Supplying the true future barrier motion still yielded only 31. Replacing the predicted robot motion with recorded motion yielded 48/48 under that diagnostic scoring procedure. Those are offline diagnostic selections, not deployable method results.

In the 31 successful selected trajectories, predicted crossing completion was 20 physics steps (83.3 ms) late. Accurate prediction of a stationary queue draining did not imply accurate timing of the subsequent accelerating motion.

The handoff failure had a different cause: queued ADVANCE commands brought the arm across the barrier, but later HOLD commands pulled it away while the original stage logic had already begun lowering or releasing. Waiting for command consistency recovered 66 candidates without canceling their queues. The other 165 still contacted the barrier during return motion.

1. Calibrate the motion, then choose when to cross

The arm grasps an object, transports it past a moving barrier under a low ceiling, and releases it at the destination. We retain a fixed IK movement and improve the decision of when to execute it.

Four development demonstrations provide the measured wrist-and-payload motion template. The measured crossing takes 40 physics steps; the old model predicts 60. Before committing, the controller uses recent barrier motion, the pending execution delay, and the candidate wait to check a crossing window. Once target commands are committed, it continues them rather than injecting a conflicting HOLD.

What remains hand-set, and what remains unsolved?

The speed factors are 0.9, 1.0, and 1.1; the added clearance is 5 mm. Candidate waits span 0–1.2 seconds at 100 ms resolution, with replanning every 50 ms. These settings are engineering choices, not a calibrated probability of safety. Four successful development demonstrations supply the motion template; the 48 test scenes do not enter calibration.

The 48 scenes combine 12 phases, two queues, and two barrier speed changes. Six HOLD scenes still fail. The simplified wrist-and-payload geometry does not cover every arm or ceiling contact, and fixed start, goal, load, and queue length limit generalization. All methods share the same handoff check, so the table does not isolate that check’s contribution. Timing includes cheap calls after commitment and excludes offline template construction.

2. Knowing the delay is not knowing the queue

In the second environment, the arm inserts a keyed plug into a socket that moves sideways. The plug is attached to the tool: this experiment isolates alignment and insertion timing rather than grasp acquisition.

Both controllers anticipate delayed execution. The distinction is whether they use the commands actually waiting to execute. In this example, delay-only prediction introduces unnecessary pauses. Full-queue prediction avoids those pauses and completes the required stable insertion within the time budget.

What is shared between the insertion controllers?

Dynamics approximation, Jacobian, thresholds, control period, and success criteria are shared. The task uses ideal analytic perception to isolate command information; it is not a visual-generalization experiment. Controller computation time is recorded but does not advance this environment’s physics clock. Completion requires the original depth, force, and stability criteria; the deadline is not relaxed for the successful clip.

3. Correcting too soon can corrupt the next correction

The third environment adds contact grasping, rotation, transport, side insertion, release, and stability verification. The object is held by friction between the fingers, not welded to the arm.

Estimating the grasp transform while the object is still responding to earlier commands mixes tracking transients with calibration error. Repeated corrections can then chase a grasp estimate that changes faster than the delayed robot can respond.

The revised controller waits until pending and issued commands agree and the object settles, remeasures the grasp transform, then sends one pose correction. It advances insertion only after alignment, and requests retreat under excessive contact. It never flushes the queue or attaches the object to the arm.

What changed across these experiments?

  1. Predict from committed execution. Use the contents and order of pending commands, not just the latest observation or a delay number.
  2. Calibrate the motion timing used by the decision. A small state-prediction error does not guarantee that the robot will reach the barrier at the predicted time.
  3. Preserve the ability to finish. Unnecessary waiting can exhaust a deadline; premature correction can destabilize calibration. Safety checks need to be evaluated alongside complete task execution.
The relevant state is not just where the robot is. It also includes what the robot is still going to do before the next decision can take effect.

What these videos do—and do not—establish

The moving-barrier result is the 48-scene calibrated-window pilot identified above. Moving insertion is a small information-ablation pilot. Side assembly is a development diagnostic. Their controllers share an execution-aware motivation but use task-specific prediction and decision rules; the three results should not be pooled into one success rate.

The selected clips make a failure mechanism visible. Aggregate results above retain unsuccessful cases. These experiments do not establish hardware performance, a universal safety certificate, or that increasing policy size improves the contribution of queue guidance.
